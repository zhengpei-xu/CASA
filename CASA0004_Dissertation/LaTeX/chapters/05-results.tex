This chapter reports the results of applying the framework from Chapter 4 to the West Midlands cycling network. Sections~\ref{sec:results-composition}--\ref{sec:results-lts} profile the classified network by facility type and LTS level. Sections~\ref{sec:results-density}--\ref{sec:results-reach} evaluate connectivity against LTN 1/20's "Cycling for all" principle through density, fragmentation, and reach analyses, and Section~\ref{sec:results-growth} compares three network growth strategies to inform upgrade prioritisation.

\section{Cyclable Network and Infrastructure Profile}
\label{sec:results-composition}

The directed cyclable network for the West Midlands comprises 23{,}301 km of edges across 480{,}228 directed links (bidirectional segments counted once per direction), derived from OS NGD Transport Network filtered by the cyclability criteria described in Section~\ref{sec:method-extraction}. Figure~\ref{fig:wm_facility_types} maps the network coloured by facility type. Mixed Traffic dominates at 18{,}935 km (81.3\%), followed by Off-Road paths at 4{,}014 km (17.2\%), comprising segments with little or no motor vehicle traffic. Active investment in dedicated cycling facilities is minimal: the three physically separated types (fully kerbed cycle track, stepped cycle track, and light segregation) total just 49.7 km, accounting for 0.2\% of the network. Paint-only provision (advisory and mandatory cycle lanes) adds a further 63.1 km (0.3\%) but offers no physical protection from motor traffic (likely an underestimate, see Section~\ref{sec:disc-osvalidation}).

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Figure/wm_facility_types.png}
  \caption{Directed cyclable network of the West Midlands Combined Authority by facility type. Where a road segment carries different provision by direction, the facility with the greater degree of separation from motor traffic is shown. The network is dominated by mixed traffic (grey) and off-road paths (purple). Physically separated infrastructure (blue) is concentrated along radial corridors into Birmingham and Coventry. }
  \label{fig:wm_facility_types}
\end{figure}

Spatially, dedicated facilities are concentrated in Birmingham, where fragments of fully kerbed track are visible along radial corridors into the city centre. Coventry, the birthplace of the modern safety bicycle, shows the second-longest continuous stretches of separated infrastructure, a product of its Local Air Quality Action Plan which funded fully segregated cycleways to avoid introducing a Clean Air Zone charging scheme \parencite{chaplin2021,coventrycitycouncil2023}. Elsewhere the picture is stark: Sandwell, Dudley, Walsall, and Wolverhampton show very little physically separated provision, and Solihull's network outside its town centre is entirely mixed traffic. Beyond segregated provision, off-road corridors such as greenways and residential streets are prevalent across the conurbation's urban areas but thin markedly toward the rural fringe, while the low-stress network elsewhere depends largely on residential streets. This spatial inequality in low-stress infrastructure provision is a recurring theme in the connectivity analysis that follows in Section~\ref{sec:results-reach}.

\section{LTS Classification}
\label{sec:results-lts}

Figure~\ref{fig:lts_map} maps the final LTS classification across the directed cyclable network after junction stress enhancement. Of the 480,228 directed edges, 227,420 (47.4\%) are classified as LTS~1, 152,180 (31.7\%) as LTS~2, 46,464 (9.7\%) as LTS~3, and 54,164 (11.3\%) as LTS~4. By network length, these correspond to 43.0\%, 34.9\%, 9.4\%, and 12.7\% respectively (Figure~\ref{fig:lts_composition}, Panel~A). The LTS~1 share consists predominantly of short, clustered residential street segments with low prevailing speeds and traffic volumes, supplemented by a smaller proportion of longer, continuous off-road links that enter the classification at LTS~1 under all link-level conditions. However, this headline figure is misleading in isolation: LTS~3 and~4, while comprising 22.1\% of total network length, form the arterial skeleton of the network, visible as a continuous grid of orange and red on Figure~\ref{fig:lts_map} that encloses the low-stress residential clusters. The implications of this structure for network connectivity are examined in Sections~\ref{sec:results-fragmentation} and~\ref{sec:results-reach}.

The aggregate distribution masks a pronounced urban--rural disparity. By network length, urban segments account for the vast majority across all stress levels (Figure~\ref{fig:lts_composition}, Panel~A), but the composition differs sharply: in urban areas LTS~3 and~4 together represent 21.6\% of network length, whereas in rural areas the equivalent share rises to 39.6\% (Figure~\ref{fig:lts_composition}, Panel~C). This disparity persists despite the classification already applying a relaxed threshold for rural mixed-traffic segments (Section~\ref{sec:method-linklts}, Table~\ref{tab:lts_matrix}). The map reveals the underlying cause: urban areas contain dense grids of low-speed residential streets that constitute the majority of network length, keeping the aggregate high-stress share low, while rural areas, concentrated in the Meriden Gap green belt\footnote{The Meriden Gap is a rural green belt area between Solihull and Coventry that forms part of the West Midlands Green Belt.} separating Solihull from Coventry, have a sparse network dominated by high-classification roads carrying speeds and volumes that place them in the highest stress categories, with few low-speed residential alternatives. The spatial distribution of this imbalance is examined through network density analysis in Section~\ref{sec:results-density}.

The distribution described above reflects the final classification after junction stress enhancement, a step that substantially reshapes the LTS profile. Prior to enhancement, 325,051 edges (67.7\%) were classified as LTS~1; afterward this falls to 227,858 (47.4\%), a reduction of 97,193 (20.3\%) edges. The overlay modifies 103,279 edges totalling 5,833.8~km (25.0\% of network length), with the majority (90,312) experiencing a one-level increase, 6,105 a two-level increase, and 7,030 a three-level increase. Uncontrolled crossings account for most upgrades (97,055 edges), reflecting the prevalence of priority junctions where cyclists must yield to or merge with motor traffic without signal protection. Roundabout approaches contribute 5,132 upgrades, typically elevating links to LTS~3 or~4, consistent with evidence that multi-arm roundabouts represent one of the most stressful cycling environments in the UK road network \parencite{davies1997,reid2011,departmentfortransport2024}. Signalised junctions produce the fewest changes, as they inherently provide temporal separation between turning motor vehicles and cyclists. The extent to which junction enhancement fragments otherwise continuous low-stress sub-networks is examined in Section~\ref{sec:results-fragmentation}.
