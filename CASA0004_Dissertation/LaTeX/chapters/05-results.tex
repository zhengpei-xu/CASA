\chapter{Results}
\label{ch:results}

% Replaced hardcoded section numbers with dynamic \ref{} commands

This chapter reports the results of applying the framework from Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} to the West Midlands cycling network. Sections~\ref{sec:results-composition}--\ref{sec:results-lts} profile the classified network by facility type and LTS level. Sections~\ref{sec:results-density}--\ref{sec:results-reach} evaluate connectivity against LTN 1/20's "Cycling for all" principle through density, fragmentation, and reach analyses, and Section~\ref{sec:results-growth} compares three network growth strategies to inform upgrade prioritisation.

\section{Cyclable Network and Infrastructure Profile}
\label{sec:results-composition}

The directed cyclable network for the West Midlands comprises 23{,}301 km of edges across 480{,}228 directed links (bidirectional segments counted once per direction), derived from OS NGD Transport Network filtered by the cyclability criteria described in Section~\ref{sec:method-extraction}. Figure~\ref{fig:wm_facility_types} maps the network coloured by facility type. Mixed Traffic dominates at 18{,}935 km (81.3\%), followed by Off-Road paths at 4{,}014 km (17.2\%), which include both purpose-built cycle routes (e.g. canal towpaths, converted railways) and paths through parks or green spaces. On-carriageway cycling provision is scarce: the three physically separated types (fully kerbed cycle track, stepped cycle track, and light segregation) total just 49.7 km (0.2\%). Paint-only provision (advisory and mandatory cycle lanes) adds a further 63.1 km (0.3\%) with no physical protection from motor traffic (likely an underestimate, see Section~\ref{sec:disc-osvalidation}).

Spatially, dedicated facilities are concentrated in Birmingham, where fragments of fully kerbed track are visible along radial corridors into the city centre. A continuous off-road corridor follows the Birmingham Canal Main Line\footnote{The Birmingham Canal Main Line is the principal canal corridor through central Birmingham and Sandwell, whose towpath functions as a shared-use walking and cycling route.} towpath westward from the city centre into Sandwell, forming one 
of the longest uninterrupted cycling routes within West Midlands. Coventry, the birthplace of the modern safety bicycle, shows the second-longest continuous stretches of on-carriageway separated infrastructure, a product of its Local Air Quality Action Plan which funded fully segregated cycleways to avoid introducing a Clean Air Zone charging scheme \parencite{chaplin2021,coventrycitycouncil2023}. Elsewhere the picture is stark: Sandwell, Dudley, Walsall, and Wolverhampton show very little physically separated provision, and Solihull's network outside its town centre is entirely mixed traffic. This uneven distribution of dedicated cycling infrastructure shapes the stress classification and connectivity outcomes in the following sections.

\section{LTS Classification}
\label{sec:results-lts}

Figure~\ref{fig:lts_map} maps the final LTS classification after junction stress
enhancement. By network length, LTS~1 accounts for 43.0\% and LTS~2
for 34.9\% (Figure~\ref{fig:lts_composition}, Panel~A). The LTS~1 share consists
predominantly of short, clustered Local Street segments, supplemented
by longer, continuous Off-Road Paths (Figure~\ref{fig:lts_composition}, Panel~D). LTS~3
and~4 together comprise 22.1\% of network length, yet form the
continuous arterial grid visible in orange and red on the map,
enclosing the low-stress clusters. Panel~D confirms a clear gradient:
A~Roads are almost exclusively LTS~4 (86.1\%), B~Roads predominantly
LTS~3--4, Minor Roads split across LTS~2--4, Local Streets largely
LTS~2 with a substantial LTS~1 component, and Off-Road Paths almost
entirely LTS~1. The implications of this enclosure pattern for
network connectivity are examined in Sections~\ref{sec:results-fragmentation} and~\ref{sec:results-reach}.

The aggregate distribution masks a pronounced urban--rural disparity:
LTS~3--4 together represent 21.2\% of urban network length but 39.5\%
in rural areas (Figure~\ref{fig:lts_composition}, Panel~C), despite relaxed rural thresholds
(Table~\ref{tab:lts_matrix}). Notably, the LTS~1 share is near-identical between
settings (43.1\% and 41.8\%). The divergence instead lies in LTS~2,
which falls from 35.6\% in urban areas to 18.6\% in rural areas,
particularly in the Meriden Gap green
belt\footnote{The Meriden Gap is a rural green belt area between
Solihull and Coventry that forms part of the West Midlands Green
Belt.}, where rural land use generates few Local Streets, the road
type that constitutes 61.1\% of total network length and is
predominantly LTS~2, and their scarcity is offset by
higher-classification roads at LTS~3--4.

The distribution above reflects the final classification after
junction stress enhancement, which substantially reshapes the LTS
profile: the LTS~1 share falls from 67.7\% to 47.4\% of edges, with
103,447 edges totalling 5,834\,km (25.0\% of network length)
modified. Uncontrolled crossings account for most elevations (97,055
edges), with roundabout approaches contributing a further 5,123
elevations, typically to LTS~3 or~4, consistent with evidence that
multi-arm roundabouts represent one of the most stressful cycling
environments in the UK road network \parencite{davies1997,reid2011,departmentfortransport2024}. Signalised junctions account for 35 elevations only. Panel~D (dotted segments) shows that Local Streets dominate
the junction elevation effect: 4,490\,km of Local Street length is
elevated from LTS~1 to LTS~2 at junctions, representing 77.0\% of
all junction-elevated network length. This confirms that uncontrolled
Local Street junctions are the primary structural barrier fragmenting
the low-stress network, a mechanism examined further in
Section~\ref{sec:results-fragmentation}.

\section{Stress-Based Network Density}
\label{sec:results-density}

Figure~\ref{fig:lts_density} presents absolute density (km/km\textsuperscript{2}) and
relative density (\% of local network length) of each LTS level
across 1,718 LSOAs in West Midlands. LTS~1 and~2 have median
absolute densities of 13.9 and 12.3\,km/km\textsuperscript{2},
substantially higher than LTS~3 (3.0) and LTS~4 (3.6), but the
spatial pattern is broadly consistent across all four levels: all
show higher values in urban areas and lower values in rural areas,
indicating that absolute density primarily captures total network
density rather than the structural prevalence of any particular
stress level. Relative density reveals more meaningful
differentiation. LTS~1 and LTS~2 have mean relative densities of
41.6\% and 37.3\%, with a combined LTS~$\leq$~2 mean of 78.9\%,
indicating that in most LSOAs over three-quarters of the local
network is low-stress. The relative density maps for LTS~1 and
LTS~3/4 show broadly inverse spatial patterns, localising the
urban--rural disparity identified in Section~5.2 to individual LSOAs.

To contextualise these findings in terms of population exposure,
Census 2021 usual resident population at LSOA level
\parencite{officefornationalstatistics2021} is joined to the density measures. Nearly
two-thirds of the region's population (64.7\%, approximately 1.89
million residents) reside in LSOAs where low-stress segments
constitute at least 75\% of local network length. This suggests that
most of the West Midlands population lives within neighbourhoods
where local streets are predominantly low-stress. However, local
density does not guarantee network connectivity: LTS~$\leq$~2
segments may be locally abundant yet disconnected from one another
by high-stress junctions and arterial barriers, a fragmentation
problem examined in Section~\ref{sec:results-fragmentation}.

\section{Stress-Based Network Fragmentation}
\label{sec:results-fragmentation}

Figure~\ref{fig:fragmentation} presents the component size
distribution across cumulative LTS thresholds. The rank-size plot
(Panel~A) shows distributions steepening markedly at lower stress
levels: the LCCs at LTS~1 and $\leq$~2 reach only 181.9 and
517.5\,km, compared to 2,972.6 and 10,634.6\,km at
LTS~$\leq$~3 and~$\leq$~4. Panel~B captures the trade-off between
network length and fragmentation: as the threshold relaxes, total
length increases while component count decreases as higher-stress
edges bridge isolated clusters. The length-weighted KDE (Panel~C)
quantifies the cyclist's experience: at LTS~1 after junction
enhancement the distribution peaks at 0.33\,km, meaning a randomly
selected LTS~1 segment typically belongs to a component spanning only
a few hundred metres. At LTS~$\leq$~2 the peak shifts to 9.1\,km,
yet most network length remains fragmented across mid-size components
rather than consolidated into a connected backbone.

Table~\ref{tab:fragmentation} reports precise fragmentation metrics
computed on the LCC of the full cyclable network (LTS~$\leq$~4,
10,634.6\,km). At LTS~$\leq$~1, the sub-network fragments into
26,176 connected components with a mean size of 0.19\,km; its LCC
spans just 181.9\,km (3.7\% of sub-network length). Relaxing the
threshold to LTS~$\leq$~2 reduces the component count to 9,637 but
the LCC share rises only to 6.5\% (517.5\,km), confirming that the
low-stress network remains severely fragmented. A qualitative
transition occurs at LTS~$\leq$~3, where the LCC absorbs
approximately a third of the sub-network (32.7\%), reflecting the
incorporation of arterial links that bridge previously isolated
clusters.

Junctions are a critical source of low-stress network fragmentation.
When junction stress is incorporated, the LTS~1 component count
increases by 39.7\% (18,732 to 26,176) while total network length
contracts by 21.7\% (6,329.0 to 4,955.6\,km), as high-stress
crossings sever links that previously connected adjacent low-stress
clusters. The LCC contracts correspondingly from 311.8 to 181.9\,km
(4.9\% to 3.7\%), and mean component size halves from 0.34 to
0.19\,km. The effect persists at LTS~$\leq$~2, where components
increase from 8,993 to 9,637 and the LCC share drops from 7.8\% to
6.5\%. These results demonstrate that the apparent abundance of
low-stress infrastructure identified in
Section~\ref{sec:results-density} does not translate into usable
continuous routes.

\section{Stress-Based Network Reach}
\label{sec:results-reach}

Figure~\ref{fig:network-reach} maps the directed network reach at the 8~km threshold across
four cumulative LTS levels. At LTS~1, reach is negligible: the median LSOA records just
0.1~km, and 37.0\% of LSOAs have zero reach entirely, reflecting the severe fragmentation
documented in Section~\ref{sec:results-fragmentation}. Relaxing to LTS~$\leq$~2 improves reach
substantially (median 10.6~km, mean 37.5~km), but 9.5\% of LSOAs still register zero reach
and the distribution remains heavily right-skewed. Spatially, higher-reach LSOAs at
LTS~$\leq$~2 align with the extended off-road corridors identified in Section~\ref{sec:results-composition},
most notably the Birmingham Canal Main Line towpath into Sandwell, confirming that low-stress
reach at this threshold depends on proximity to a small number of continuous traffic-free
routes. A qualitative shift occurs at LTS~$\leq$~3 (median 61.3~km, 4.2\% zero reach), and
at LTS~$\leq$~4 the network delivers near-universal coverage. The contrast between
LTS~$\leq$~2 and $\leq$~4 quantifies the connectivity penalty imposed on stress-averse
cyclists: the median reach at LTS~$\leq$~2 is just 0.7\% of that available to cyclists
willing to tolerate all stress levels.

While network reach quantifies how far a cyclist can travel from any given point, it does not
capture whether specific destinations are actually reachable. To test this, two demand-oriented
analyses (Figure~\ref{fig:od-connectivity}) are applied, each targeting a user group central to
the LTN~1/20 ``cycling for all'' principle: short-distance school accessibility for children
(LTS~1) and commuting for mainstream adults (LTS~$\leq$~2). At LTS~1, only 4.1\% of the
region's population can reach their nearest school via a route within the stress threshold
deemed suitable for children. LTS~1 school access is scattered across urban LSOAs with no
distinct clustering, arising where schools happen to fall within short residential or off-road
segments. At LTS~$\leq$~2, school accessibility reaches 65.4\%, but over a third of the
population remains without a low-stress school route. The commuting analysis reinforces this:
of inter-MSOA OD pairs within 8~km straight-line distance, only 13.0\% of commuting flow is
connected at LTS~$\leq$~2, and those connected pairs face a median detour of
1.76$\times$ the unconstrained shortest path (flow-weighted mean 2.46$\times$). Spatially,
LTS~$\leq$~2 connectivity is highest in Sandwell, Dudley, and western Birmingham, reflecting
the continuous off-road corridors, but remains near zero across southern Birmingham, Solihull,
and Coventry. A qualitative transition occurs at LTS~$\leq$~3, where 50.8\% of flow becomes
connected with a reduced median detour of 1.49$\times$. These results indicate that the
network fails to deliver adequate low-stress connectivity for either school trips or commutes;
even where low-stress routes exist, substantial detours roughly double journey distance
relative to unconstrained routing.

\section{Network Growth}
\label{sec:results-growth}

As set out in Section~\ref{sec:method-growth}, the three strategies
select upgrade edges by different logics: Random draws uniformly from
the candidate pool, Connectivity prioritises edges that merge distinct
components into the LCC, and Betweenness Centrality (BC) selects edges
with the highest topological demand proxy. Figure~\ref{fig:growth_curves} presents the
growth curves for all three strategies across the full candidate pool.
For LCC share, connectivity reaches 0.85 within approximately
2{,}000\,km of upgrades, whereas Betweenness Centrality requires
approximately 2{,}800\,km and Random approximately 3{,}300\,km. The
separation is more pronounced for demand-weighted connectivity metric:
connectivity reaches 0.6 at approximately 1{,}500\,km, while Betweenness Centrality and
Random achieve only 0.28 and 0.01 respectively at the same upgrade
length. Figure~\ref{fig:growth_curves} zooms into the first 100\,km, corresponding to a
realistic short-term investment horizon. The divergence between
strategies is starker at this scale. Connectivity improves steadily
from the first step. Betweenness Centrality strategy shows almost no
change in the first 20\,km before beginning a slower ascent and Random
remains near the baseline throughout.

All three strategies converge toward similar terminal values (LCC
share 0.86, demand-weighted connectivity 0.71), but the paths to
convergence differ substantially. The upgrade length required for
connectivity to reach an LCC share of 0.8 is roughly half that
required by Random. In any realistic budget-constrained planning
horizon, the choice of selection logic determines not whether the
network eventually becomes connected, but how quickly meaningful
connectivity is achieved.
