\chapter{Discussion}
\label{ch:discussion}

This chapter interprets the results in relation to the research aims and wider literature. Section~\ref{sec:disc-cyclingforall} evaluates the West Midlands network against LTN 1/20's "cycling for all" principle, situating the connectivity findings within evidence on cycling demand and mode share. Section~\ref{sec:disc-junction} examines junction stress as a structural mechanism of low-stress network severance, distinguishing the barrier roles of crossings and roundabouts. Section~\ref{sec:disc-planning} draws implications from the growth simulation for investment prioritisation and cross-authority coordination. Section~\ref{sec:disc-osvalidation} assesses OS NGD cycle lane data through a first independent comparison with OpenStreetMap, and Section~\ref{sec:disc-limitations} discusses limitations and directions for future work.

\section{Evaluating ``Cycling for All'': Network Connectivity Against LTN 1/20 Objectives}
\label{sec:disc-cyclingforall}

LTN 1/20 established as its first principle that cycle infrastructure should be accessible to everyone ``from 8 to 80 and beyond'', and operationalised this through its CLoS and JAT tools for individual scheme appraisal \parencite{departmentfortransport2020a}. This study tests that principle at network scale by introducing an LTS classification drawn directly from CLoS, JAT and design criteria within LTN 1/20, mapping nationally defined suitability tiers onto distinct user groups from children cycling independently (LTS 1) through mainstream adults (LTS 2) to the most confident cyclists (LTS 3 and 4). The connectivity indicators this framework produces are therefore not judgements imposed by an external standard but LTN 1/20 measuring itself, answering in quantitative terms how far its own ``cycling for all'' principle is realised across the West Midlands.

The connectivity results are consistent with observed cycling behaviour in the region. The West Midlands records a cycling mode share of approximately 0.9\% \parencite{westmidlandscombinedauthority2024}, within a national context where cycling accounts for only 2\% of all trips in England, a figure essentially unchanged since 2002 \parencite{departmentfortransport2025}. The network evaluation offers a structural explanation for this stasis: low-stress reach contracts so severely that the network accessible to stress-averse cyclists is negligible relative to the full cyclable network, leaving the vast majority of commuting journeys---the most spatially regular component of travel demand and therefore the most amenable to infrastructure-led mode shift---either unconnectable at tolerable stress levels or viable only through detours that eliminate cycling's competitive advantage over driving.

\textcite{cervero2019}, modelling cycling commuting across 36 cities and towns in Britain, found that the diversion penalty between low-stress and shortest paths was a significant negative predictor of cycling mode share, and that corridor-level LTS composition was directly associated with commuting rates, with each 10-percentage-point increase in very low stress links corresponding to a 0.65 percentage point increase in cycling. Rather than indicating that low cycling is purely a matter of cultural preference or car dependence, these findings suggest that network structure is at minimum consistent with, and plausibly contributes to, the region's low cycling uptake. By LTN 1/20's own standards, the West Midlands remains far from providing the connected, direct, low-stress routes required to support ``cycling for all''.

\section{Junction Stress and the Structural Severance of the Low-Stress Network}
\label{sec:disc-junction}

The Conveyal-lineage approach that dominates applied LTS practice assigns the highest segment LTS at a vertex to all incident edges, without distinguishing travel direction or crossing conditions. The directed classification developed here, grounded in LTN 1/20's Junction Assessment Tool, propagates stress only to edges whose cyclist confronts the conflicting traffic. It further applies roundabout-specific criteria that existing implementations lack, capturing the near-absolute barrier that standard roundabouts pose to low-stress continuity. These design-guidance-consistent criteria reveal how higher-order traffic conditions propagate through intersections to sever low-stress connectivity, making the structural barrier role of junctions observable at network scale.

Junction stress reclassifies a modest share of total network length yet fragments the low-stress network to a degree far beyond that proportion. This reflects the structure of the low-stress network itself: low-stress segments form short, clustered residential streets connected to one another through a small number of nodes where they intersect with higher-classification roads, and these nodes are precisely where the speed and volume of crossing arterials trigger junction stress. Junction stress therefore does not act uniformly across the network but concentrates at the topological bottlenecks between clusters, severing connections that cannot be replaced by alternative low-stress routes and producing a fragmentation effect far exceeding what the share of reclassified length alone would suggest. It is thus best understood as the network expression of arterial severance rather than a consequence of junction form alone, especially when physically separated infrastructure is largely absent. Arterial roads impose high stress both along their links and at the points where residential streets must cross them; the junction analysis captures this second dimension, which link-level classification would miss and the Conveyal-lineage approach would overstate.

Junctions, whether through the incremental erosion of crossings or the near-absolute severance of roundabouts, thus account for the structural disconnection of the low-stress network. Identifying effective junction-level interventions is therefore central to any strategy for restoring connectivity. The finding that signalised junctions produce the fewest reclassifications is consistent with route choice research demonstrating that traffic signals substantially reduce perceived crossing stress for cyclists \parencite{broach2012}. Junction-level interventions such as signal control and crossing islands therefore constitute a mechanism for reconnecting low-stress clusters at their topological bottlenecks, at a fraction of the scale of corridor-level reconstruction.

\section{Network Growth and Implications}
\label{sec:disc-planning}

The growth simulation operates on the LTS-classified network developed in this study, where connectivity is defined by continuous low-stress conditions rather than by physically kerbed cycling infrastructure alone. Its central finding is that a strategy targeting edges which merge disconnected components outperforms one based on betweenness centrality in improving the connectivity of a fragmented low-stress cycling network. This is consistent with \textcite{nateraorozco2020}, who found that component-bridging strategies were effective across 14 cities (including London) with fragmented cycling networks.

However, while the connectivity strategy proves effective across both network definitions, the betweenness centrality strategy reveals a different pattern, showing near-zero connectivity improvement in the first 20\,km. Because centrality is computed on the full street network, the highest-ranked edges tend to be arterial roads embedded within clusters of other high-stress segments; upgrading them in isolation creates disconnected low-stress sections rather than bridging existing components. This mechanism likely explains the betweenness centrality strategy's slower performance across the full growth trajectory, suggesting that strict demand-proxy strategies which do not account for the structure of the existing low-stress subnetwork may systematically misidentify upgrade priorities in highly fragmented networks.

The random strategy produces almost no measurable connectivity improvement within the first 100\,km, establishing a lower bound on uninformed investment. While real-world scheme selection is not random, network-level coordination across local authority boundaries remains a recognised challenge in practice. Discussions with practitioners involved in West Midlands transport planning indicated that although technical delivery standards are consistent across authorities, political appetite for active travel investment varies substantially between them, creating uneven implementation along corridors that span multiple local authority boundaries. The contrast between random and connectivity illustrates the value of network structure information: even a simple strategy that targets component-bridging edges substantially outperforms one that ignores network topology entirely.

LTN 1/20 advocates demand-based network planning, including mesh density analysis to identify gaps and the use of origin--destination data to understand travel patterns \parencite{departmentfortransport2020a}. However, the techniques it provides do not evaluate how individual scheme upgrades interact to produce network-level connectivity outcomes, and the operational tools that determine funding eligibility---CLoS and JAT---evaluate individual schemes in isolation. The simulation results suggest this gap has measurable consequences: at network scale, the order in which edges are upgraded shapes the trajectory of connectivity growth as much as the total investment itself.

\section{Assessing OS NGD Cycle Lane Data: A First Independent Comparison with OpenStreetMap}
\label{sec:disc-osvalidation}

OpenStreetMap (OSM) is increasingly central to cycling infrastructure research \parencite{ferster2020,nelson2021,vidal-tortosa2026}, and UK-based studies are no exception \parencite{yeboah2015,cervero2019,lovelace2020}. However, OSM's decentralised contribution model produces known tagging inconsistencies across regions and time periods \parencite{ferster2020,lukawska2026}, and key attributes for more granular infrastructure assessment, such as cycle lane width and directionality, are either absent from or inconsistently recorded \parencite{wasserman2019,lukawska2026}.

The UK, uniquely, now offers an authoritative alternative. The release of OS NGD Transport cycle lane data provides the first nationally consistent cycling infrastructure dataset with facility descriptions aligned to LTN 1/20 terminology. The dataset records cycle lane width (modal and minimum), direction of travel, and facility type as native attributes. It further integrates with the wider OS NGD ecosystem, providing RAMI average speed, elevation gain, road classification, road structure, and junction type. Its uniform national capture specification \parencite{ordnancesurvey2025} eliminates the cross-regional inconsistencies that complicate multi-area comparisons in OSM-based cycling research.

Despite these design strengths, the dataset has not yet been independently assessed, and its completeness directly conditions the reliability of all downstream LTS results. To examine this, OS NGD Cycle Lane Feature Type records were compared against OSM-derived cycling infrastructure for Birmingham. The comparison reveals a category-specific pattern. For physically separated infrastructure, OS NGD records 66.3\,km against 57.2\,km in OSM (ratio 1.16), with the additional capacity to distinguish fully kerbed cycle tracks, stepped cycle tracks, and light segregation where OSM cannot differentiate beyond a single ``track'' category. However, for paint-only cycle lanes (advisory and mandatory), the pattern reverses: OS NGD records 25.5\,km against 71.5\,km (ratio 0.36), capturing barely one-third of OSM's paint-only provision while exceeding it on separated infrastructure.

This divergence is consistent with OS's documented capture methodology: cycle lanes are ``sourced from OS aerial imagery'' and features not visible from above ``will not be included'' \parencite{ordnancesurvey2025}. Physically separated tracks produce clear aerial signatures through kerbs and barriers; paint-only markings do not, and OSM's advantage here reflects street-level contributor knowledge that a national aerial survey systematically misses. However, this gap has negligible implications for the LTS results, as paint-only lanes offer no physical protection and share near-identical classification boundaries with mixed traffic under the thresholds developed in Section \ref{sec:method-linklts}. As the current release represents the first full national coverage version of the dataset, the paint-lane gap may diminish as subsequent maintenance cycles refine data quality. Despite the systematic paint-only undercounting, the completeness gap is category-specific and predictable from the capture methodology: OS NGD captures what is visible from above and misses what is not, a pattern that future releases can address without redesigning the dataset's architecture.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:disc-limitations}

Two data limitations bear directly on the reliability of the link-level classification. Firstly, the CLoS and LTN 1/20's design criteria specify traffic volume thresholds using both AADT and HGV composition, the latter reflecting evidence that HGVs are disproportionately involved in cyclist fatalities \parencite{morgan2010}, with HGV involvement identified as a significant independent risk factor in overtaking collisions in the UK \parencite{chao2025}. The absence of network-wide vehicle composition data prevented the application of these HGV thresholds, potentially underestimating stress on high-freight-traffic routes. Secondly, the 85th percentile speed used throughout the classification was estimated from OS NGD average speed data by applying a fixed multiplier that assumes a normal speed distribution \parencite{nationalhighways2019}. On roads where traffic calming or congestion produces skewed distributions, this assumption may misrepresent prevailing speeds.

The junction stress classification adopts the JAT's movement-specific logic, assigning crossing stress to directed approach edges through bearing-based conflict detection. This improves upon the \textcite{conveyal2015} method's undirected max-propagation by identifying which approach directions face conflicting traffic. However, the primal-graph representation means that different turning movements from the same approach edge receive the same junction stress value, whereas in practice a right turn involving no crossing and a through movement traversing a high-stress leg carry very different stress. \textcite{furth2026}, led by the original author of the LTS framework, address this through a dual-graph representation where each turning movement is an independent edge with its own stress value, enabling routing algorithms to evaluate junction stress at the level of individual movements along a path. Integrating this approach with the LTN 1/20-grounded classification developed here represents a direction for future work.

The network growth simulation is subject to several simplifications. Upgrade is modelled as a uniform binary operation, whereas LTS 4 arterials typically require substantially greater investment than LTS 3 residential streets. Betweenness centrality is pre-computed as a static ranking and does not adapt as the low-stress subnetwork evolves. The simulation also operates exclusively on link-level classification without modelling junction-level interventions; given the role of junction stress in fragmenting the low-stress network, link upgrades alone may overestimate achievable connectivity gains. Finally, the connectivity strategy's greedy expansion concentrates investment near the existing largest connected component, potentially neglecting peripheral communities, a spatial equity dimension identified by \textcite{szell2022} and \textcite{zhao2023} that neither evaluation metric captures.

These limitations notwithstanding, the framework's grounding in LTN 1/20 and reliance on nationally scoped data, particularly OS NGD Transport, which has achieved full cycle lane coverage across Great Britain, means the same methodology can be applied to any English city without recalibrating thresholds or substituting data sources. This would produce cross-city comparable connectivity indicators under a unified national standard, enabling systematic benchmarking of how far different cities have progressed toward LTN 1/20's objectives. More fundamentally, the classification developed here inherits LTN 1/20's design thresholds; its validity therefore depends on the standard itself, not solely on this operationalisation. The individual thresholds LTN 1/20 specifies each draw on specific safety evidence, but their combination into a unified stress classification has not been systematically validated against cyclist-perceived stress. Future research could test this correspondence through stated-preference surveys, revealed route choices, or GPS trajectory analysis, evaluating the underlying design standard rather than the implementation presented here.
